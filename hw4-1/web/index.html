Project 4 · Part A

IMAGE WARPING and MOSAICING

Overview

Setup and Conventions

In this part of the project I stitched photos into a mosaic. I shot photos from a fixed point, recovered the homography between each pair, warped one image into the other's frame with my own inverse warping, and blended the results. I didn't use any of the built-in homography or warping functions (cv2.findHomography, cv2.warpPerspective, etc.). I wrote computeH, both interpolation methods, and the blending myself, using only NumPy for the linear algebra.

The originals are $5712 \times 4284$. I downscaled them by $0.25$ to $1428 \times 1071$ before processing, which keeps the warps fast and the mosaics a reasonable size. OpenCV loads images in BGR order, so I convert to RGB before plotting with matplotlib. I treat pixel $(0, 0)$ as the center of the top-left pixel, which makes the interpolation arithmetic simpler.

Part A.1

Shoot the Pictures

For this project, I took multiple images of San Francisco skyline to build the image stitching based on. I kept in mind to stay in the same position and take photos as close together in time as possible so panorama stitching looks convincing in the result. Also, I kept in mind to overlap images, so I have information I can use to glue images together. Also, to test out the effect of zooming in the image stitching, I took images at different focal lengths.

For the transform between two photos to be a pure homography, the camera has to rotate about its center of projection without translating. If it moves, nearby objects shift relative to far ones (parallax), and no single homography can line them up. The skyline set was shot from a hilltop, so almost everything is far away and close to a plane at infinity. The only nearby things are the trees and the dirt slope in the foreground.

I took ten photos at Sather Gate within about 30 seconds, standing in one spot and turning the camera, some at different zoom levels. To choose a pair for the mosaic, I ran my matching on all 45 pairs and compared how many correspondences survived. I used sather9 and sather10. They overlap heavily (1127 inliers), and together they cover the whole gate. Some pairs scored higher but were worse choices: one image was mostly a zoomed-in part of the other, or the second image added only sky.

Part A.2

Recover Homographies

A homography maps a point $p = (x, y, 1)$ to $p' = Hp$, where $H$ is $3 \times 3$. Since $H$ is only defined up to scale, I fix $h_{33} = 1$, leaving 8 unknowns. Writing out $u = \frac{h_{11}x + h_{12}y + h_{13}}{h_{31}x + h_{32}y + 1}$ (and the same for $v$) and multiplying through by the denominator makes each correspondence $(x, y) \to (u, v)$ give two linear equations:

$$\begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -ux & -uy \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -vx & -vy \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ \vdots \\ h_{32} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}$$

computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) stacks these rows for all $n$ points into $A h = b$. With $n = 4$ there is exactly one solution, but a few pixels of error in any point changes $H$ a lot. With $n > 4$ the system is overdetermined, so I solve it in the least-squares sense with np.linalg.lstsq.

Correspondences. Instead of clicking points by hand, I detected SIFT features in both images (cv2.SIFT_create) and matched them with Lowe's ratio test (0.75). For the San Francisco pair, that gave 963 candidate matches. Some of these are wrong, and others sit on the nearby trees, which don't follow the same homography because of parallax. To drop them, I wrote a RANSAC loop around my own computeH. It fits $H$ to 4 random matches, counts how many matches land within 2 px, and keeps the largest such set. That left 767 inliers, and refitting $H$ on all of them by least squares gives a mean reprojection error of 0.87 px (max 2.80 px). I also wrote select_points.py, a ginput clicking tool. Any hand-clicked points it saves are used in place of the automatic matches.

I warp image 2 into image 1's frame, so the system maps points in image 2 to points in image 1. Here are the first three correspondences' rows of $A h = b$ (all 767 points give 1534 rows):

$$\begin{bmatrix} 5.099 & 627.7 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -4197 & -516700 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 5.099 & 627.7 & 1 & -3579 & -440500 \\ 20.89 & 587.9 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -17340 & -488100 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 20.89 & 587.9 & 1 & -13980 & -393400 \\ 11.20 & 654.1 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -9312 & -544100 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 11.20 & 654.1 & 1 & -8109 & -473700 \\ & & & & \vdots & & & \end{bmatrix} h = \begin{bmatrix} 823.2 \\ 701.8 \\ 830.1 \\ 669.1 \\ 831.8 \\ 724.3 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

The recovered homography:

$$H = \begin{bmatrix} 0.3237 & 0.2259 & 721.7 \\ -0.3033 & 0.9367 & 151.2 \\ -0.000511 & 0.0000843 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The large $h_{13} \approx 722$ is the horizontal shift: image 2 lands to the right of image 1. The non-zero bottom row is the perspective part. It's what makes image 2 grow taller toward its far edge once warped.

For Sather Gate, the ratio test kept 1257 matches and RANSAC kept 1127 inliers, with a mean reprojection error of 0.90 px (max 2.31 px). Nearly all the inliers are on the gate and the trees behind it. None are on the people walking through, because they moved between the two shots and don't fit the homography. The first rows of the system:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 452.5 & 542.2 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -2031 & -2434 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 452.5 & 542.2 & 1 & -129500 & -155100 \\ 455.5 & 620.5 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -2394 & -3262 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 455.5 & 620.5 & 1 & -167300 & -227900 \\ 456.0 & 600.6 & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -2968 & -3909 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 456.0 & 600.6 & 1 & -158300 & -208500 \\ & & & & \vdots & & & \end{bmatrix} h = \begin{bmatrix} 4.5 \\ 286.2 \\ 5.3 \\ 367.4 \\ 6.5 \\ 347.2 \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$$

The recovered homography:

$$H = \begin{bmatrix} 1.393 & -0.01755 & -615.3 \\ 0.1577 & 1.363 & -455.4 \\ 0.000311 & 0.000181 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The diagonal entries of about $1.39$ mean sather10 has to be enlarged by roughly 1.4× to match. In other words, sather9 was taken more zoomed in, which a homography handles without any extra steps. The negative $h_{13}$ moves sather10 to the left of the reference.

Part A.3

Warp the Images

I used inverse warping. Pushing every source pixel forward through $H$ leaves holes wherever the image gets stretched. Instead, for every output pixel I apply $H^{-1}$ to find where it came from in the source, then sample the source there.

To size the output, I pass the four corners of the source image through $H$ and take their bounding box. I then left-multiply $H$ by a translation (translate_homography) so the box starts at $(0, 0)$. Each warp returns a 4-channel image, and the extra channel is an alpha mask that is 1 where the output pixel has a value and 0 where it falls outside the source. The blending step uses this mask.

The first version looped over pixels in Python, which took minutes on the full-resolution photos. The final version is vectorized. np.meshgrid builds every output coordinate at once as a $3 \times N$ homogeneous array, a single matrix multiply by $H^{-1}$ maps them all, and I divide by the third coordinate.

  • warpImageNearestNeighbor rounds each source coordinate to the nearest pixel center and copies that pixel.
  • warpImageBilinear takes the four surrounding pixels and averages them with weights $(1-d_x)(1-d_y)$, $d_x(1-d_y)$, $(1-d_x)d_y$, $d_x d_y$, where $d_x, d_y$ are the fractional offsets. I didn't use any library interpolation.

At full size the two look almost the same, but zoomed in the difference is clear. Nearest neighbor gives blocky, stair-stepped edges on rooftops and windows, because neighboring output pixels often copy the same source pixel. Bilinear is smoother, but slightly softer, since every output pixel is an average.

For speed, on the $\approx 11$ megapixel warp canvas nearest neighbor took 0.74 s and bilinear took 1.66 s, about 2.2× slower. Bilinear reads four pixels and computes weights instead of one lookup. Bilinear is worth that cost for final results. Nearest neighbor is fine for quick previews, or for warping masks and labels where values shouldn't be averaged.

Rectification

To test the warp on a single image, I click the four corners of an object I know is rectangular (top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left). I map them to a rectangle with the object's known aspect ratio, using the clicked top edge's length as the width. Then I compute $H$ with computeH and warp the whole image with warpImageBilinear.

Part A.4

Blend the Images into a Mosaic

I left image 1 unwarped and warped image 2 into its projection. The mosaic canvas is the bounding box of image 1's corners together with image 2's warped corners. Both images are warped onto this one canvas, image 1 by the identity plus the canvas offset, so their alpha masks line up.

Pasting image 2 on top of image 1 would leave a hard edge wherever the two differ in exposure or alignment. So I blend them in two steps:

  1. Feathering. For each image, a distance transform of its alpha mask (cv2.distanceTransform) gives each pixel's distance to that image's nearest edge. I normalize the two distances into weights $w_1, w_2$ that sum to 1 and take the weighted average. Each image dominates near its own center and fades out linearly toward its edge.
  2. 2-level Laplacian pyramid. Feathering alone leaves "ghosts," where small details are slightly misaligned or moved between shots and show up twice at half strength. So I split each image into a low band (Gaussian blur, $\sigma = 4$, computed only over valid pixels so the black background doesn't bleed in) and a high band (image minus low band). I feather the low band with $w_1, w_2$. For the high band, I take each pixel from whichever image has the larger weight there. Smooth color and brightness changes blend gradually, and sharp details come from only one image.

Mosaic 1: San Francisco

The registration is accurate. Streets, buildings and the shoreline continue across the overlap with no visible doubling, which matches the sub-pixel reprojection error. The two shots were taken seconds apart with similar exposure, so the houses themselves blend without a visible seam. The close-up shows where blending does matter: the brown hillside in the bottom-left. It's close to the camera, so because of parallax it doesn't sit in the same place in the two photos. Overwrite just replaces it with image 2's houses. Feathering averages the two, so the hillside fades in as a see-through layer over the rooftops, the same kind of ghosting as a moving object. The Laplacian blend keeps the houses sharp, but the hillside ends at a visible edge where the high frequencies switch from one image to the other. No homography can align both the distant city and the nearby hill at once.

The mosaic is strongly stretched toward the right. The two photos overlap by only about a third, and the camera turned a long way between them. Projecting image 2 onto image 1's flat image plane enlarges it more and more the farther it is from image 1's center. With more overlap, or by warping both images to a middle projection or onto a cylinder, the stretching would be much smaller. The trees in the top right and the slope in the foreground are close to the camera. Any parallax would show up there first, but because RANSAC fit $H$ to the distant city, the far scene is what lines up.

Mosaic 2: Sather Gate

The gate lines up well. The arch, both lamp posts and the pillar carvings continue across the overlap without doubling. This pair shows the difference between the blending methods much more clearly than San Francisco, because people walked through the gate between the two shots. In the overwrite version, the person in front of the pillar is cut off at the seam. With feathering, that person becomes a see-through ghost over the pillar, since the two images are averaged where one has a person and the other doesn't. The Laplacian blend mostly removes the ghost, because each pixel's sharp detail comes from only one image. A faint tint remains where the person's color leaked into the low band.

The black regions come from the two images covering different areas: sather10 includes more sky and the left side of the gate, and sather9 includes more of the plaza and the right. Cropping to the largest filled rectangle would remove them.
