Project 4

IMAGE WARPING and MOSAICING, Feature Matching for Autostitching

Overview

Setup and Conventions

In this project, I learned how to stitch photos into a mosaic. I shot multiple photos from a fixed point, recovered the homography between each pair, warped one image into the other's frame, and blended the results. I implemented the homography solver, both warps, and the blending from scratch, using NumPy for the linear algebra. In Part A, the only OpenCV feature tool I used was SIFT, to find candidate point matches. In Part B, I replaced SIFT with my own pipeline (Harris corners, ANMS, patch descriptors, ratio-test matching, and RANSAC) to stitch the same images fully automatically.

Part A.1

Shoot the Pictures

For this part of the project, I took multiple images of San Francisco skyline, Sather Gate, and my dorm Unit 2 to build the image stitching based on.

I kept in mind to stay in the same position (fix center of projection) and take photos as close together in time as possible so panorama stitching looks convincing in the result. Also, I tried to take images that have overlaps, so I have information I can use to glue them together. To test out the effect of zooming in the image stitching, I took images at different focal lengths as well.

Part A.2

Recover Homographies

In this step, I implemented computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) function.

A homography maps a point $p = (x, y, 1)$ to $p' = Hp$. We get 8 unknowns, and I fix $h_{33} = 1$ to remove the scale ambiguity. Each point correspondence gives two linear equations below.

$$\begin{bmatrix} x & y & 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & -ux & -uy \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & x & y & 1 & -vx & -vy \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} \\ h_{12} \\ \vdots \\ h_{32} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix}$$

computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) stacks these rows for all $n$ points into $A h = b$. With $n = 4$ there is exactly one solution, but a few pixels of error in any point changes $H$ a lot. With $n > 4$ the system is overdetermined, so I solve it in the least-squares sense with np.linalg.lstsq.

Correspondences Visualized

Instead of clicking points by hand, I found the correspondences automatically. I detected SIFT features in both images (cv2.SIFT_create) and matched them with Lowe's ratio test (0.75). Some of those matches are wrong, or sit on nearby objects that don't follow the same homography because of parallax. To remove them, I wrote a RANSAC loop around my own computeH: fit $H$ to 4 random matches, count how many matches land within 2 px, and keep the largest such set. The final $H$ is the least-squares fit to all of those inliers. I didn't use cv2.findHomography or any other built-in homography function. I also wrote select_points.py, a ginput clicking tool; any points saved with it replace the automatic matches.

Part A.3

Warp the Images

In this part of the project, I implemented warpImageNearestNeighbor(im, H) and warpImageBilinear(im, H) using inverse warping. To do so, I broke down the task into the following three parts:

1. Bounding Box Computation

To size the output, I pass the four corners of the source image through $H$ and take their bounding box. I then left-multiply $H$ by a translation (translate_homography) so the box starts at $(0, 0)$. Each warp returns a 4-channel image, and the extra channel is an alpha mask that is 1 where the output pixel has a value and 0 where it falls outside the source. This mask is saved to be used in the blending task!

2. Inverse Coordinate Mapping

np.meshgrid builds every output coordinate at once as a $3 \times N$ homogeneous array, a single matrix multiply by $H^{-1}$ maps them all, and I divide by the third coordinate.

3. Scratch Interpolation

  • warpImageNearestNeighbor rounds each source coordinate to the nearest pixel center and copies that pixel.
  • warpImageBilinear takes the four surrounding pixels and averages them with weights $(1-d_x)(1-d_y)$, $d_x(1-d_y)$, $(1-d_x)d_y$, $d_x d_y$, where $d_x, d_y$ are the fractional offsets. I didn't use any library interpolation.

At full size the nearest neighbor approach and the bilinear approach outputs something almost the same. However, if zoomed in, nearest neighbor gives blocky with stair-stepped edges on rooftops and windows, whereas bilinear is smoother and slightly softer, since every output pixel is an average.

For speed, on the $\approx 11$ megapixel warp canvas nearest neighbor took 0.74 s and bilinear took 1.66 s, about 2.2× slower. This is because bilinear reads four pixels and computes weights instead of one lookup.

Rectification

In the step of implementing image warping, I test the functions on a single image that I know is rectangular. I mapped the corners to a rectangle with the object's real aspect ratio, using the length of the top edge as the width. Then I compute $H$ with computeH and warp the image with warpImageBilinear. I found each corner by zooming into the full-resolution photo and reading off its pixel coordinates. Lastly, to avoid points near the horizon get stretched without limit and the predicted bounding box to be enormously large, I clip the output to two rectangle sizes around the target.

Part A.4

Blend the Images into a Mosaic

To merge two images to produce a stitched panoramic photo, I left image 1 unwarped and warped image 2 into its projection. The mosaic canvas is the bounding box of image 1's corners together with image 2's warped corners.

Pasting image 2 on top of image 1 would leave a hard edge wherever the two differ in exposure or alignment. I tried Feathering and 2 level Laplacian pyramid to avoid this issue.

Method and Implementation Logics

  1. Feathering. For each image, a distance transform of its alpha mask (cv2.distanceTransform) gives each pixel's distance to that image's nearest edge. I normalize the two distances into weights $w_1, w_2$ that sum to 1 and take the weighted average. Each image dominates near its own center and fades out linearly toward its edge.
  2. 2-level Laplacian pyramid. Feathering alone leaves "ghosts," where small details are slightly misaligned or moved between shots and show up twice at half strength. So I split each image into a low band (Gaussian blur, $\sigma = 4$, computed only over valid pixels so the black background doesn't bleed in) and a high band (image minus low band). I feather the low band with $w_1, w_2$. For the high band, I take each pixel from whichever image has the larger weight there. Smooth color and brightness changes blend gradually, and sharp details come from only one image.

Even though I took photos in a short period of time, there are some errors when stitching images together caused mainly by change in angle or the movement of the subject that makes it hard to stitch images seamlessly. To mitigate this, the 2-level Laplacian pyramid came in clutch to get rid of "image ghosts". For example in Mosaic 2, there is a face of male student walking in the middle of the image that looks semi-transparent due to the feathering process of image blending. The Laplacian method blends only the blurred (low-frequency) parts of the two images smoothly, and takes sharp detail from just one image at each pixel, so this "ghost" became a lot less visible and made the blending seamless.

Part B.1

Harris Corners and ANMS

In this part of homework, I replaced SIFT with my own feature pipeline inspired by "Multi-Image Matching using Multi-Scale Oriented Patches" (Brown et al.). This process includes a few simplifications:

  • single scale
  • no sub-pixel refinement
  • no rotation

Harris corners: Following the provided harris.py, detector smooths the image gradients' outer products with a Gaussian ($\sigma = 1$) to get the second-moment matrix $\mathbf{H}$ at every pixel. The corner strength is the harmonic mean of its eigenvalues, $f_{HM} = \det \mathbf{H} / \operatorname{tr} \mathbf{H}$, and every local maximum in a $3 \times 3$ window counts as a corner. Corners within 20 px of the border are dropped so the 40×40 descriptor window in B.2 always fits.

Adaptive Non-Maximal Suppression: As you see in the photos on the left side, when outputting all Harri's codners, this algorythm returns tens of thousands of corners, mainly crowded into textured areas like trees and gravel. As we are interested in these Harris corners as key points, I just kept the strongest ones. ANMS helps me doing so by giving each corner a suppression radius, the distance to the nearest corner that is clearly stronger.

$$r_i = \min_j \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert \quad \text{s.t.} \quad f(\mathbf{x}_i) < c_{\text{robust}} \, f(\mathbf{x}_j), \qquad c_{\text{robust}} = 0.9$$

In this imprementation, I kept the 1000 corners with the largest radii. A corner survives if it is the strongest in a large neighborhood, so the strongest corners are evenly over the image. To make this fast, I sort the corners by strength first. The corners that can suppress corner $i$ are then exactly a prefix of the sorted list, so I only compare each corner against that prefix, in chunks to optimize memory usage.

Part B.2

Feature Descriptor Extraction

In this step, I impremented Feature Descriptor extraction. For each corner, I describe the area around it with an axis-aligned version of the MOPS descriptor.

  1. Blur - I blur the grayscale image with a Gaussian ($\sigma = 2.5$) so each sample in the next step stands for its whole 5×5 area instead of a single pixel. This helps me avoid aliasing.
  2. Sample - I take an 8×8 grid of samples, 5 px apart, spanning the 40×40 window centered on the corner. All corners are sampled at once with NumPy fancy indexing.
  3. Bias/gain normalize - I subtract the patch mean and divide by its standard deviation, so every descriptor has mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (normalization). Two views of the same spot then match even if one photo is brighter or has more contrast.

The descriptors keep the coarse structure while dropping fine detail.

Part B.3

Feature Matching

In this step, I computed the squared distance between every descriptor in image 1 and every descriptor in image 2 using the provided dist2.

For each feature in image 1, I find its nearest neighbor (error $e_{1\text{-NN}}$) and second-nearest neighbor ($e_{2\text{-NN}}$) in image 2. The reason why I kept track of second nearst neighbor is that a single threshold on $e_{1\text{-NN}}$ works poorly because some features are distinctive and others are not. I use Lowe's ratio test, and a match is kept only if its best match is much better than the runner-up.

$$\frac{e_{1\text{-NN}}}{e_{2\text{-NN}}} < 0.4$$ (I chose 0.4 from Figure 6b of the paper)

Almost all correct matches have a ratio below about 0.4, while incorrect matches spread between about 0.6 and 1. If a feature looks similar to many others (sky, repeated windows), its first and second neighbors are about equally close and the ratio test rejects it.

Part B.4

RANSAC for Robust Homography

I implemented 4-point RANSAC from scratch in ransac_homography in the following steps:

  1. Pick 4 random matches and compute the exact homography through them with my Part A computeH.
  2. Map every matched point in image 2 through that $H$ and count the inliers, the matches that land within 3 px of their partner in image 1.
  3. Repeat 5000 times and keep the largest inlier set.
  4. Refit $H$ to all of those inliers with least squares.

A wrong match almost never agrees with the homography that the correct matches share, so it falls out of the consensus set. I then build the mosaic with the same warp and 2-level Laplacian blend as Part A.4.

For San Francisco and Sather Gate, the automatic homography is within about 0.25 px of the Part A one, and the mosaics look the same. That is with far fewer correspondences (36 and 162, compared with 767 and 1127), because ANMS spreads them over the whole overlap.

San Francisco needed one adjustment. With the paper's 500 ANMS corners, only a handful of corners fell in the small overlap, and several RANSAC inliers were on the clouds, which drifted between the two shots and pulled $H$ off. Keeping 1000 corners put enough matches on the city for the static scene to win the vote.

The dorm pair is the weakest, with only 17 inliers and a 6 px error, even though the mosaic still looks close to the Part A one. The Part A homography shows the second view is rotated by roughly 15°. My descriptors are axis-aligned, so a rotated patch produces a different descriptor and few features match. Adding the paper's rotation invariance (orienting each patch along its dominant gradient) would fix this.
